# The four pillars — full rules

Source: Learn By Leo, "How to edit SO good your viewers get addicted to your videos"
(youtu.be/sLgHqZSe2o0, 2024). His first three uploads made 300K+ views and 13K subs
with no promotion. Paraphrased rules, with his reasoning attached so the agent can
apply judgment instead of copying tricks.

## 1. The hard truth — editing serves the experience

- A great editing style can bring perfect watch time, or bad views. It depends on fit.
- **Sam Sulek case:** a 26-minute car-and-gym vlog with only 17 cuts (about 90 s
  between cuts) and no other editing, and he has millions of views. He *did* edit: he
  cut every part the viewer didn't come for. His viewers want to feel like they're
  hanging out with someone. Quick cuts and flashy animation would ruin that.
- **MrBeast** is the opposite: rapid-fire for viewers who want entertainment.
- **Rule:** disrupting what the viewer came for is the best way to make them leave.
- **Tradeoff:** cutting every pause so the speaker never stops talking is the single
  biggest entertainment lever, and it costs authenticity.

### Pacing targets by experience (agent heuristic; Leo gives only the two endpoints)

| Experience | Typical niches | Avg seconds per shot | Graphics density |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hang-out / authentic | vlogs, fitness diaries, podcasts | 30–90 s | almost none; cut only the boring parts |
| Education / explainer | how-to, YouTube growth, finance, tech | 3–8 s | high: B-roll + motion graphics carry the explanation |
| High-stimulation entertainment | challenges, stunts, kids, commentary | 1–3 s | very high, plus constant audio stimulus |

Leo's own video sits in Education. The face-cam was on screen for roughly a quarter
of the frames sampled from his video, and the rest was timeline recordings, graphics
and examples.

## 2. Healthy variety of visuals

**Why:** a sudden change in what you see grabs attention, on screen as in life. A
typical edit with only bad takes, pauses and tangents removed may show just 3
different things in a minute. That's boring.

**Footage types:**
- **A-roll** (subject seen and heard together): personal, holds attention when spoken
  with confidence. Use it in short bursts, for important lines.
- **B-roll** (footage recorded separately from the audio): shows what you're saying,
  so ideas land clearer and faster than a face would. Use it as much as possible.
  Variants:
  - Stock footage: the "lazy version".
  - **Motion graphics:** the biggest gain in explanation speed. Dude Perfect example:
    an "essential but boring" puzzle explained in seconds by animation. A-roll would
    have been too slow and live B-roll too confusing.

**The drawback: visual mush.** Cutting as often as possible makes the video
confusing, which hurts engagement. Don't minimise shot length for its own sake. A clip
can stay 10 s if it's interesting for 10 s.

**Spice without cutting:**
- Slow scale or position drift on any still or boring clip. Better still, a
  perspective (3D tilt) move.
- Abrupt punch-in zoom on A-roll when saying something important ("listen up").
- Wiggle/shake keyframes: Leo doesn't like them, so treat them as optional.
- Overlays on top of footage.

**Captions:** not filler. Captions used just to add visual variety get obnoxious and
waste a slot a better visual could use. Use them to make the viewer notice specific
words. If there's truly nothing else, captions beat nothing. **Max 3 words on screen
at a time.**

**Guiding attention inside an image.** Showing a whole image plainly is boring and
confusing. Lead the eye to the focus point:
1. Animate the key text or area being highlighted.
2. Darken or blur everything around the focus point.
3. Hue shift: red for negative, yellow or green for positive.
4. Circles, arrows and underlines.
5. Make the subject glow.
6. Scale and position moves (the baseline).

**Make it look good:** colour grade, vignette, subtle particle animation.

## 3. Visual continuity — the "digital morphine stream"

Goal: a seamless feed with no rough edges where distraction can build up. The editing
should be invisible and the experience mesmerising.

- **No magic appearances.** A graphic must move into the frame. If it does just
  appear, explain the arrival with a sound (camera shutter, pop).
- **The eye-trace rule (most crucial):** at a cut, the viewer's focus point in the
  last frame of clip A should be where the focus point is in the first frame of
  clip B. A jump from one side of the screen to the other is jarring and breaks
  immersion. If every cut does that, the video becomes unwatchable.
- **Full-screen transitions** help smooth cuts. Keep them subtle.
- **Break continuity on purpose:** a deliberate jolt gets attention back. Use it
  rarely.

## 4. Immersive audio — the other half of the video

Sound design means the viewer experiences every cut, transition, animation and piece
of footage through a second sense. It doubles the stimulation and steers mood.

**Base layer:** sound effects wherever they make sense:
- Something moves → whoosh.
- Something gets highlighted → highlight sound.
- Something appears → pop or shutter.

**Emotion hijackers** (don't overuse any of them, or they lose their meaning):
- **Risers** build tension and anticipation that something important is coming. Use
  one only if something important really does follow, or it stops working.
- **Hits** release tension and make a moment feel important. Use alone or after a
  riser.
- **Drones** add mystery and intrigue. Save them for darker moods and underlying
  suspense.

The more emotionally invested the viewer is, the more engaged they are.

**Music:**
- Spend real time on choosing it; fit matters a lot.
- Split the video at subject changes and give each segment the mood the viewer should
  feel. Leo uses "anticipation" tracks so the viewer always feels something big is
  coming, and "innovative" tracks for the groundbreaking-advice segments.
- **Abrupt stop:** jolts the viewer and spotlights the moment. Use it for special
  beats.
- **Slow fade-out:** tells the viewer a segment is closing, which builds anticipation
  for what comes next.
- **Sync the song's hits and dips to the edit:** e.g. the music picks up exactly when
  the video moves from problem to solution. This marks the topic shift and makes the
  solution feel exciting.
- **Stems:** download the separate song elements (Epidemic Sound offers this) to drop
  or add layers per segment.
- Sources: YouTube Audio Library and free artists for a start; a licensed library
  (Epidemic Sound, Envato Elements) once you're serious. SFX: freesound.org.

## Leo's suggested learning path (from the video description)

1. Pick an editor (he uses Premiere Pro + After Effects).
2. Watch a 30–60 minute basics tutorial for it; knowing many effects gives you
   creative freedom.
3. Learn keyframe animation.
4. Learn masking.
5. Learn effects and plugins (e.g. the AE Glow effect, Video Copilot's Saber).
